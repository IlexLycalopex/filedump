02 — Point of view

Most finance leaders do not lose money on the ERP project they ran. They lose it on the one they deferred.

Deferral feels prudent because its cost never lands on a single line of the P&L. It is spread across late closes, rekeyed data, workarounds that only one person understands, and decisions made a fortnight after they were needed. None of it appears on a business case, which is precisely why it survives.

My argument to a CFO is not that transformation is easy. It is that the comparison is rarely stated honestly. Put a number against the status quo, phase the change so it can be paused rather than abandoned, and the decision becomes commercial rather than emotional.

That is also why we run vertical ERP-selection sessions rather than generic demos. Buyers arrive with a live problem, and they leave with a comparison they can defend to a board.

  • How long does your month-end actually take? Not the reported figure. The point at which finance stops correcting it.
  • How much of your finance team is rekeying? Invoice handling, order entry and reconciliation are usually the first things automation repays.
  • What does a new entity, site or country cost you? If the answer is a project, the system is now a constraint on the growth plan.
  • Who is the single point of failure? Most mid-market finance functions have one. Key-person risk is a system problem, not an HR one.
  • Could you evidence your numbers under audit tomorrow? Compliance and reporting obligations keep tightening. Spreadsheet chains do not.
